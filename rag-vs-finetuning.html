Your data · plain-language guide

Three ways to give a model your data.

People argue about which one wins. They aren't answering the same question. The useful question is much smaller: where does your data enter — the weights, part of the prompt, or all of the prompt?

RAGRetrieve it into the prompt. The data stays outside the model. At the moment of the question, the right few pieces get fetched and pasted in.
FINE-TUNINGTrain it into the weights. You show the model a few thousand worked examples, and it comes out doing the job that way by default.
LONG CONTEXTJust paste it all in. No index, no training run. The whole corpus goes into one prompt and the model reads it fresh, every time you ask.

Three places, three bills, three failure modes. Nearly everything else on this page follows from which one you picked — and from the fact that mature systems pick all three, for different jobs.

Step 1

The three mechanisms, one paragraph each

No maths, no vendor names. Just what physically happens to your documents in each case.

RAG — retrieve it into the prompt

Your documents get cut into pieces, each piece turned into a vector, and the vectors stored. When a question arrives you search that store for the pieces closest in meaning, paste the winners into the prompt, and ask the model to answer from them. The model learns nothing and keeps nothing — it just gets handed the right page at the right moment.

Four tiles cover the moving parts: chunking, embedding, the vector store, and reranking — the second pass that throws most of the candidates away. The whole thing is RAG.

Fine-tuning — train it into the weights

You assemble a dataset of input-and-ideal-output pairs and run a short training pass on top of an already-trained model. The weights move a little. What comes out is a model that does your version of the task without being told how, every time, with no instructions in the prompt.

In practice almost nobody moves all the weights — LoRA trains a small adapter beside them instead, which is why a fine-tune is now an afternoon rather than a datacentre.

Long context — paste it all in

Skip the machinery. Put the entire handbook, the whole contract, all forty files in one prompt and ask your question at the end. Modern context windows are large enough that this is a real option for a surprising number of tasks, and it takes no engineering at all.

It is the only one of the three with no build step, no index to keep fresh, and no training job. That is its entire pitch, and it is a good one — right up to the point where you ask the same question twice.

The middle lane is the only one where the data has an address at answer time — you know exactly which pieces you sent, so the answer can point at them. The top lane dissolved your examples into weights. The bottom lane sent everything and let the model find it.

One consequence worth naming early: in the middle lane you can filter before the model sees anything. Retrieval can run as the asking user, so a document they aren't allowed to read is never fetched. Neither of the other two lanes has that seam — weights don't know who is asking, and a pasted corpus is all-or-nothing.

Step 3

What each one is actually for

This is where most of the confusion lives, and it clears up fast once you notice the three are answering different questions. One handles what the model knows, one handles how the model behaves, and one handles a job you only do once.

RAG is for facts

Specifically: facts that are fresh, private, or too big to fit. Yesterday's incident report. The customer's contract. A wiki that four people edit daily. Anything past the model's knowledge cutoff, and anything it was never allowed to see.

It is also the only one of the three that can show its work, which is why it is the standard answer to hallucination. Not a cure — the model can still misread a source — but a retrieved answer comes with the source attached, and a wrong one is checkable in seconds.

Fine-tuning is for behaviour

Format, tone, a domain's conventions, the shape of a task. A model that always returns your five fields in your order. A model that writes release notes the way your team writes release notes. A classifier that has internalised the fifteen edge cases your policy document argues about.

The test: could you get this by writing a very long prompt with a dozen examples in it? If yes, a fine-tune does the same thing permanently, with a shorter prompt and less drift. If no amount of prompting gets you there, a fine-tune is the honest next step.

Long context is for the bounded one-off

The corpus fits, and you ask about it once — or a handful of times, by a person, in one sitting. Review this contract. Summarise these forty tickets. Find every place this repo talks about retries.

Building an index for that is engineering you throw away. Paste it in, get the answer, close the tab. The moment it becomes a feature that thousands of requests hit, the arithmetic flips — see the second half of the next section.

Said in one line each: RAG changes what the model sees. Fine-tuning changes what the model is. Long context changes neither — it just sends more.

Correction · Figure 2

Fine-tuning is not how you teach a model your wiki

This is the single most expensive misunderstanding in the space, and it is completely reasonable to hold. "Training" sounds like teaching, teaching sounds like facts, and every vendor page uses the word "custom". So teams spend a quarter building a dataset out of their documentation, fine-tune on it, and get back a model that sounds exactly like their documentation and gets the details wrong.

The reason is in where knowledge comes from. Nearly all of a model's factual knowledge arrives in pretraining — the enormous run over a corpus you can't see. The much smaller pass afterwards mostly reshapes behaviour: following instructions, answering rather than continuing, refusing some things. Your fine-tune sits at the end of that line and is smaller again. Against the weight of pretraining, four thousand wiki pages are a rounding error — enough to teach the register of your wiki, not the contents of it. The pretraining vs post-training tile is the one-sentence version.

Facts that change belong on the bottom row, where updating one means editing one document. Put them on the top row and updating one means rebuilding a dataset and retraining a model.

Three practical consequences fall out, and each one is a reason on its own:

Expensive to update

A fact in a document is edited in a text box. A fact in weights is edited by rebuilding the training set and running the tune again — and you can't remove one fact, only retrain without it. Every correction is a release. Every deletion request is a release.

Impossible to verify or cite

A tuned fact has no address. There is no chunk to point at, no line to quote, no way to answer "where did that come from?" other than trusting the output. And a half-absorbed fact is stated with exactly the same confidence as a solid one — that is what hallucination is.

No permissions, no freshness

Weights don't know who is asking. Anything tuned in is readable by every user of that model, and no filter can put it back. It is also frozen at the moment you trained — a second knowledge cutoff, this one of your own making.

To be fair to the mechanism: a fine-tune can memorise, given enough repetition of the same fact. That's precisely why the misconception survives — the demo works. The claim here isn't that it's impossible. It's that it is the most expensive, least verifiable, hardest-to-update way to store a fact that a text file stores for free.

The other half: long context is not free

The mirror-image mistake is newer and spreading faster: windows got big, so paste everything, always. Two things quietly go wrong.

You pay for it on every request. Billing counts tokens in and tokens out, not questions — see token pricing. A 300-page handbook in the prompt is 300 pages billed per question, forever, including all the pages irrelevant to that question. Prompt caching takes a real bite out of this when the pasted block is byte-identical and sits at the front — that is the correct optimisation and you should use it — but it only discounts a repeated prefix, and it does nothing for the second problem.

Accuracy slides as the prompt grows. Fitting inside the window is the low bar, not the goal. A model handed an enormous prompt does not attend to it evenly; the material buried in the middle is the likeliest to be missed, and the effect gets worse as you add more. That is context rot, and it means "just send everything" can be less accurate than sending the right eight paragraphs. Retrieval isn't only a cost optimisation. It is frequently a quality one.

Step 5 · the concrete version

What a training example actually teaches

The abstract argument lands harder as two short files. On the left, one row of a fine-tuning dataset. On the right, the prompt a retrieval system assembles. Look at what the highlighted part of each is teaching.

fine-tuning · one row of the dataset 
{
  "messages": [
    { "role": "user",
      "content": "Summarise ticket 8812" },
    { "role": "assistant",
      "content": "SEV2 · billing · EU
        Impact:  3 tenants, no data loss
        Owner:   payments
        Next:    rerun the refund job" }
  ]
}

A thousand of these teach one thing: the shape. Not what is in ticket 8812 — the model will not reliably recall that. The house format for summarising any ticket, applied without being asked.

RAG · the prompt that actually gets sent 
system:
  Answer only from the sources below.
  Cite the id of every one you use.

sources:
  [kb-231] EU refunds settle in 3-5
           working days after approval.
  [kb-118] Approval is automatic under
           the 500-unit threshold.

user:
  How long does a small EU refund take?

The fact arrives at request time, with an address. Edit kb-231 tonight and tomorrow's answer changes — nothing retrained, nothing redeployed, and the answer can name its source.

Now swap them and watch both fail. Fine-tune on ten thousand tickets to teach the facts of ticket 8812, and you get fluent summaries of tickets that don't exist. Retrieve your style guide into every prompt to teach the format, and you pay for the style guide on every request while the model half-follows it — because a document describing a format is not the same as a thousand demonstrations of it.

Figure 3

They don't compete — they stack

The versus framing is the artefact of a headline. A serious system uses all three at once, because it has all three problems at once: it needs a consistent output shape, it needs current facts, and it needs the working set of the current task in view.

The division of labour is clean: fine-tune for the output shape, retrieve for the facts, and spend the context window generously on the working set — the open document, the last few turns, the rows the user just filtered to.

Three colours, three mechanisms, one request. The teal path changes hourly, the orange node changes quarterly, and the purple middle is decided fresh on every turn. Nothing here is a substitute for anything else.

Notice the ordering that falls out of this. Retrieval and context are things you tune every week; a fine-tune is a thing you ship every few months. Building the slow one first, before you know what the fast ones can already do, is how teams end up with an expensive model trained to do something a better prompt would have handled. Which is the whole reason the decision section below has a strong default.

Decision · Figure 4

So which should you use?

Five questions, in order. Most systems get their answer in the first two.

1 · Does the answer change when your data changes?

If editing a document should change tomorrow's answer, the data has to live outside the weights. That is retrieval, and it is not a close call.

2 · Is it private, or per-user?

If two users must get different answers from the same system, you need a filter at request time. Only retrieval has one. Weights are shared by everyone who calls the model.

3 · Does the answer need a citation?

Anyone in support, legal, medicine or finance answers yes without thinking. A cited answer requires that you know what you sent — retrieval, again.

4 · Is it about what it knows, or how it answers?

"How" is the fine-tuning question — and even then, try the prompt first. Fine-tune when a well-written prompt with examples still can't hold the shape, or when the prompt needed to hold it has become absurd.

5 · Does the whole thing fit, and do you ask once?

Both true: paste it in and go home. Either false — it doesn't fit, or you'll ask a thousand times — and you're back to retrieval.

Two of the four exits cost you almost nothing to try. Take those first, and only spend the quarter on a fine-tune once the cheap exits have been genuinely ruled out — with evals, not with a feeling.

And the sentence that settles most arguments before they start: if the answer changes when your data changes, it is retrieval. Everything else is a refinement of that.

Quick answers

The questions everyone asks

"Can I fine-tune to add knowledge?"

Technically yes, practically no. With enough repetition a tune will memorise, but you get a fact you can't update without retraining, can't cite, can't scope to a user, and can't delete. Compare that with editing a text file. The mechanism isn't broken — it's just the wrong storage.

"Is RAG dead now that context windows are huge?"

No, and the reason isn't nostalgia — it's the bill and the accuracy curve. Big windows killed the fiddly RAG people built when nothing fit: aggressive summarising, tiny chunks, elaborate compression. What survives is simpler and still wins — send the right material rather than all of it. Big windows made RAG easier, not unnecessary.

"Do I need a vector database?"

Often not. RAG means "retrieve, then generate" — it never specified how. Your existing full-text search, a SQL query, or a plain file listing all count, and for a few thousand documents keyword search is frequently better because people search with exact terms. Reach for a vector store when meaning-based matching is doing real work, and expect the best setups to run both and merge.

"Which do I try first?"

RAG, almost always. It's the fastest to build, the easiest to debug — you can read exactly what you sent — the only one you can fix by editing a document, and it improves the moment the underlying model does. Try it first even when you suspect the answer is a fine-tune: a working retrieval pipeline is how you find out what the model still gets wrong, which is the dataset your tune would have needed anyway.

"Isn't fine-tuning cheaper at scale?"

It can be, and that's a legitimate reason to do it — a tune that removes two thousand tokens of instructions and examples from every request pays for itself at volume. Note what that argument is about: shorter prompts, not better knowledge. Check prompt caching first, since it attacks the same cost without a training run.

"How do I know if it worked?"

The same way for all three: a fixed set of real questions with known-good answers, scored before and after. Without evals you can't tell a better retriever from a luckier day, and this is exactly where a fine-tune goes wrong quietly — the output looks more like yours while being no more correct.
