Model selection · decision guide

Choosing a model: the axes, not the leaderboard.

Every team asks the same question — which model should we use? — and almost every answer they get is a model name. The name is the one part of that answer guaranteed to expire.

THE QUESTION PEOPLE ASK"Which model is the best one right now?" It has a new answer every quarter, and answering it leaves you with nothing you can reuse the next time it changes.
THE QUESTION THAT DECIDES"What is the cheapest, fastest model that passes twenty examples of my actual task?" Same procedure every quarter — and the answer is yours, not a reviewer's.

What doesn't expire is the shape of the decision: five axes you trade against each other, and one procedure for picking a point on them. That's the whole page.

Ground rule

Why there are no model names on this page

No model names. No prices. No benchmark scores. That isn't modesty about the state of the art — it is the argument, so it gets said out loud rather than quietly obeyed.

Model names, per-token prices and context limits move on a quarter's timescale. The axes they move along have not changed since the first chat API shipped: things that are more capable cost more and take longer, and someone has to run the hardware. Advice pinned to the first list is wrong by the time you read it. Advice pinned to the second is still useful in two years.

The second reason matters more. A page that tells you which model to use trains you to re-ask the question every time the leaderboard moves — which is often, and which is somebody's afternoon every time. A page that tells you how to decide leaves you holding something that answers it again for free: a small set of your own examples and a rule for reading them. If a claim would need updating in a quarter, it isn't on this page.

Figure 1

Five axes, and why you can't max them all

Every model decision — every one, on every menu, in every year — is a point in the same five-dimensional space. Naming the axes is most of the work, because the argument in the room is usually two people optimising different ones without saying so.

Capability

How hard a task it lands without hand-holding: messy input, several steps, an unfamiliar format, an instruction it has to hold onto for eleven turns. This is the axis people mean when they say "better", and the only one you can't buy with engineering.

Cost per request

What one unit of traffic costs, multiplied by the traffic you hope to grow into. It's billed in tokens in and tokens out — see token pricing — so a long prompt isn't a one-time design decision, it's a recurring bill on every call forever.

Latency

Two numbers that people quote as one: time to the first token, and time to done. A person watching a cursor is judging the first. A pipeline is judging the second. They rank models differently.

Context length

How much you can put in front of it in one go — the context window. A bigger window is not free headroom: everything you put in it is read, paid for, and competing for the model's attention with everything else in there.

Openness and control

Who holds the weights, and therefore who decides when the behaviour changes — open-weights vs closed. It's the axis that looks like a philosophy question and turns out to be an operations and unit-economics question.

Everything on the left is something a stakeholder will ask for. Everything on the right is the invoice. The one dotted arrow is the only discount on the board, and it comes with a condition attached — an idle GPU bills exactly like a busy one.

The mechanics behind the arrows are dull and reliable. Capability comes from a bigger model, from more tokens spent thinking before answering, or from both — and both are paid in money and milliseconds. Context is read on every request, so a window you actually fill shows up twice on the bill: once in tokens, once in the wait. Openness moves you from renting answers to renting hardware, which is a better deal only above a volume you have to actually measure.

The honest caveat: the whole frontier moves outward every generation. This year's cheap model does what last year's expensive one did, and that is a real and large effect. What doesn't move is the shape — at any single moment, on any single menu, the trade is still in front of you. That's why this page is about the shape and not about the menu.

The axis people argue about

Open-weights vs API, in plain words

OPEN-WEIGHTSYou get the weights and run them yourself. Control over the version, a data boundary you draw, and per-GPU-hour economics — paid for with operations you now own.
APISomeone else runs it. Zero ops, elastic capacity, and usually the top of the capability range — paid for per request, on terms and a schedule you don't set.

One correction before the argument, because it's the most common wrong sentence in this whole topic: open-weights is not open source. What you get is a downloadable set of weights, usually under a licence with conditions attached. What you almost never get is the training data or the pipeline that produced them — which means you cannot rebuild the model, audit what went into it, or fix a problem at its source. You can run it, adapt it with LoRA, and shrink it with quantization. That is genuinely a lot. It is not "software you can build from source", and blurring the two hides the one property people actually care about: whether the thing can change under you.

Control — the version stops moving

Weights on your disk don't get deprecated, retired, or silently improved. You choose the upgrade date, you rerun your evals against it, and you roll back if it's worse. On the API side the model is a supplier-controlled input: pin what you can, and treat the pin as part of your contract.

Privacy — the request never leaves

For some data this is the entire decision and the other four axes are tie-breakers. It's also the cleanest reason to self-host, because it's the one you can state in a sentence to a compliance officer rather than defend with a spreadsheet.

Unit economics — you rent hardware, not answers

Self-hosting swaps a per-token bill for a per-hour one on GPUs. At steady, high volume the two curves cross and open wins by a lot. At spiky or low volume they don't come close, because you pay for the idle hours too. The crossover is about utilisation, not about how big your company is.

Zero ops — the line nobody costs

The API side's real product is that capacity planning, upgrades, batching, quantizing, and being paged at 3am are someone else's job. Add those back and self-hosting is a headcount, not a line item. Teams under-price this exactly once.

And then the part the framing usually misses: these two stack rather than compete. Plenty of serious systems run both — a small open model inside the boundary for the bulk of traffic, an API frontier model for the hard minority that justifies the trip. The interesting question is almost never "which side are we on". It's "which slice of my traffic goes where", which is the next section.

Figure 2 · the practical one

Most of your traffic is easy — the flagship is for the minority

This is the most useful fact about production traffic, and almost nobody designs around it: the work is not uniformly hard. Classifying a ticket, pulling three fields out of a form, rewriting a sentence, deciding which of five tools to call, summarising something short — a small model lands those all day, every day, without drama.

The hard minority is real too: the ambiguous case, the long multi-step chain, the one that needs actual reasoning about a domain. That's what the flagship is for, and it's usually a small share of the volume carrying a large share of the value. Send everything to the flagship and you pay flagship prices for the easy majority — which is the difference between a feature that pays for itself and one that quietly dies at the next budget review.

The escalation arrow is what makes this safe to build: the cheap path is allowed to fail, because failing costs one extra call rather than one wrong answer. Without it, routing is just a quality cut with better branding.

Three mechanisms exist because of this shape. They're worth knowing by name, because each answers a different version of "the small model isn't quite good enough".

Routing — choose per request

Model routing picks the model per call rather than per product, with an escalation path when the cheap one isn't confident (retries and fallbacks). The router is itself a component that can be wrong, so it needs its own eval — otherwise you've moved the failure rather than removed it.

Distillation — teach the small one

Distillation turns a big model's outputs into training data for a small one. Narrow is the operative word: you're buying flagship-grade behaviour on one task, not in general, and the moment the task widens the small model is back to being a small model.

Quantization — same model, fewer bits

Quantization stores weights at lower precision so the model is cheaper and faster to serve. The quality cost ranges from unmeasurable to unacceptable depending on how far you push it — which is precisely why you measure it on your own examples rather than on somebody's chart.

Before changing models at all, two levers cost nothing in capability. Prompt caching stops you paying full price for a preamble that is identical on every call. Batch processing trades latency you weren't using for a cheaper rate on work nobody is waiting for. Most of what gets called cost engineering is levers like these — the model choice is one line in that discipline, not the whole of it.

Figure 3

Latency is a product decision, not a benchmark number

Latency gets quoted as one number and it is two: time to the first token, and time to done. Which one matters is decided by what the person is doing while they wait — which is a product fact, not a model fact, and it belongs to you rather than to the model card.

That's why streaming is the cheapest latency win available. It doesn't make the model faster; it makes the wait legible. A model that starts writing immediately and finishes in eight seconds usually feels faster than one that sits in silence for four and then drops a paragraph. Same axis, different product.

Both rows can be the right answer. Putting the bottom row behind a chat box is the mistake — the silence a batch job doesn't mind is the exact thing a waiting person experiences as "broken".

A reasoning model is the bottom row on purpose: it spends tokens thinking before it answers, trading latency and cost for correctness on hard problems. That's frequently a great trade — for a plan, a code change, an analysis that runs overnight. It is a bad trade in an interactive chat, and no amount of prompt tuning converts one row into the other.

So the order is: set the latency budget from the product first, then let it eliminate candidates. A two-second budget and a model that thinks for thirty seconds isn't a tuning problem, it's a mismatch. And remember the axis interaction from Figure 1 — a long context is a delay you pay on every single request, not just a capability you bought once.

The point of the page · Figure 4

How to actually decide: twenty examples and a rule

Here is the whole procedure. It is smaller than the meeting it replaces, and it is the reason this page can refuse to name a model: the procedure doesn't change when the models do.

1 · Collect twenty real examples. From your own traffic if you have it, from the closest thing you have if you don't. Real means ugly: the truncated input, the wrong language, the customer who wrote three questions in one sentence, the case the team argues about. Twenty invented, tidy examples measure only whether the model can do the easy version — which every candidate can.

2 · Write the pass rule before you run anything. One line: what makes an output right. Make it as machine-checkable as you can stand — a field that matches, a schema that parses, a decision that equals the one a human made. If you can't write that rule, you don't yet know what you're buying, and no model is going to tell you.

3 · Run candidates cheapest first. The first one that passes is your model. Not the best one that passes — the first. Everything above it is capability you're paying for and not using.

4 · Keep the set. Rerun it when the prompt changes (prompt versioning exists for exactly this), when the provider ships an update, when someone proposes a switch. This is an eval — a small one, and small is the point. The comprehensive version is the one that never gets built.

The loop only ever climbs, and only when it has a reason. That single property is what stops a system from defaulting to the biggest thing on the menu — which is what happens whenever the decision has no denominator.

Why leaderboards don't transfer

A public benchmark is an average over someone else's distribution of tasks, and usually over tasks selected because they separate strong models from each other. Your task is one point, not an average — and typically a narrow, format-heavy point: does it emit the JSON your parser accepts, in your domain's vocabulary, calling your tools, inside your latency budget, on your ugliest inputs.

A model can top every board and fail your twenty without anything being broken. That's not a paradox; it's what "different task" means. There's a second effect too: once a benchmark is public it becomes a target, and a measure that becomes a target stops being a measure. Use leaderboards to build the shortlist. Never to close the decision.

Why "we tried it and it felt better" is the expensive answer

Vibes have no denominator. You remember the two impressive answers and not the eighteen ordinary ones. You compare a freshly-tuned prompt on the new model against last quarter's prompt on the old one. And the comparison that would show the cheap model already passing has no champion in the room — nobody's reputation improves by proving the smaller thing was fine.

So the answer is always the bigger model, every time, and the bill compounds quietly. That is how teams end up paying over and over for capability they can't point at a single request that used it. The twenty examples exist to give the cheap option a way to win an argument — which, without them, it structurally cannot.

Decision

So which should you use?

Start at the bottom, not the top

Pick the cheapest model that could plausibly work, run your twenty, and move up only when it fails. The reverse never happens in practice: nobody proposes downgrading a system that already works, so starting at the top means staying there forever.

One model is a starting point, not an architecture

Most systems that run for a year end up with two or three — a small one for the majority, a strong one for the hard minority, sometimes a reasoning one for work that runs offline. Design the seam early. Retrofitting a router into code that assumes one model touches every call site.

Choose openness for a reason you can name

Steady high volume, data that can't leave, or a version you must freeze. "We might want control someday" is not one of the three, and the hardware bills either way. If none apply, rent — and revisit the moment utilisation changes.

Set the latency budget before you compare quality

It's the fastest filter you have and it comes from the product, not the model. Decide what the user is doing while they wait, then delete every candidate that can't meet it — before anyone runs a single quality comparison on it.

One question settles most of the remaining doubt: can anyone point at a request from last week that actually needed the model you're paying for? If yes, you're on the right point of the curve and the cost is doing work. If nobody can find one, you're paying for a benchmark result your users never touch.

Quick answers

The questions everyone asks

"Is the biggest model the safe default?"

It's the expensive one. It is safe only in the narrow sense that it's rarely the reason something failed — so the failure you get instead is a cost line nobody can explain and a latency budget you can't meet, both of which arrive later and are harder to reverse.

Real safety here is cheap to buy properly: twenty examples through the small model first. If it passes, the biggest model was never protecting you from anything.

"When does self-hosting pay off?"

Three reasons, and you should be able to name which one is yours: volume that is high and steady, data that can't cross a boundary, or a version you must freeze.

The economics are a fixed cost against a per-request one, so the crossover is about utilisation, not company size — a GPU you keep busy is cheap, one you keep warm for spiky traffic is not. Then add the ops line: capacity, upgrades, quantizing, on-call. That's a headcount, and leaving it out of the sum is how self-hosting "pays off" on paper and not in the budget.

"Do benchmarks transfer to my task?"

Directionally, weakly. They're an average over someone else's tasks, and once published they become a target that everyone optimises against. Both effects push the same way: the gap between board position and your result is unpredictable in size and in sign.

Use them for the shortlist — they're genuinely good at telling you which three candidates to test. Then let your own examples close it. Your eval has one advantage no leaderboard can have: it's your distribution.

"How many examples does an eval need?"

As a rule of thumb: twenty to eliminate, and a few times that to separate two candidates that are genuinely close. Twenty catches the format failures, the domain failures, and the flat "it can't do this at all" cases — which is most of what actually kills a candidate.

Twenty real examples beat two hundred invented ones, every time. And if two models tie at twenty, that's a result too, not a stalemate: take the cheaper and faster one.
